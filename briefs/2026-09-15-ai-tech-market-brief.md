**Why bond yields matter for stocks:** When bond yields rise, the "risk-free" return from holding US government debt becomes more attractive. That makes stocks look more expensive by comparison, especially high-valuation tech stocks whose profits are far in the future. A higher discount rate (the rate used to calculate the present value of future cash flows) reduces the value of those future earnings today.

## 8. Building Your Knowledge

- **Ceasefires are not peace treaties.** The Ukraine energy truce is a limited, tactical agreement, not a permanent settlement. Wars end through negotiation, exhaustion, or decisive victory — not through single-issue agreements. The "frozen conflict" risk is that both sides use the pause to rearm.
- **Bond yields and stock prices are inversely correlated in a rate-hiking cycle.** When yields rise, stocks (especially high-growth tech) typically fall because higher discount rates reduce the present value of future earnings. The S&P 500's recent rally despite rising yields is unusual and may indicate the market believes the Fed's hawkishness is transitory.
- **Open-weight AI models are creating a commoditization race.** The Mozilla report's key finding — 4-month head start at 5x cost — is a benchmark to watch. If the gap continues to shrink, it changes the investment thesis for both frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic) and the chip companies that depend on the most advanced training infrastructure.
- **Export controls are a two-way street.** South Korea restricting chip equipment and China restricting yttrium are both examples of "economic warfare." The country that controls a critical choke point (rare earths, advanced lithography, HBM memory) gains leverage. The risk for investors is that these weaponized supply chains break unpredictably.
- **The distinction between "open weights" and "open source" matters for regulation.** Open weights (the trained parameters) let you run and fine-tune a model, but they don't give you the training code or data. The Open Source Initiative argues that "open weights" is not truly open source. This distinction will influence future laws: should a model be regulated based on how it was made or what it does? Today, it's mostly about what it does.
- **When a central bank is expected to raise rates, watch the "Fed dot plot" (their projections for future rates).** The rate decision itself is often already priced in. The market moves on the *forward guidance* — whether the Fed signals one hike and done, or a series of hikes. The CNBC survey shows 75% probability of another hike in December, which is more hawkish than "one and done."

## 9. Foundations & Terms

### Concept Spotlight: Duration (Bond Duration)

**What it is in plain words:** Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in interest rates. Think of it as the "weighted average time until you get your money back." A bond with a duration of 5 years will fall roughly 5% in price for every 1 percentage point (100 basis points) rise in interest rates. Similarly, it will rise 5% for every 1 percentage point drop.

**Why it exists / what problem it solves:** Bond investors need a single number to compare risk. A 10-year bond and a 2-year bond both pay coupons, but they react very differently to rate changes. Duration normalizes this: you can compare a 10-year zero-coupon bond (duration = 10) to a 5-year bond with coupons (duration ~4.5). It tells you which bond is riskier in a rate-hiking environment.

**A concrete worked example with real numbers:** Today, the 10-year Treasury yield hit 5% (source 94). Suppose you bought a 10-year Treasury note one year ago when the yield was 4%. The bond's price would have fallen because yields rose. If the bond's duration is 8.5 years (a typical value for a 10-year coupon bond), then a 1 percentage point yield increase would cause roughly an 8.5% price decline. Your bond lost 8.5% of its market value, though its face value ($1,000) is unchanged. If you hold to maturity, you get the face value back, but if you sell now, you take a loss.

**Rough benchmarks worth remembering:** A 2-year Treasury note has a duration around 1.9 years. A 30-year Treasury bond has a duration around 12-15 years. Higher duration = higher interest rate risk. In the current environment, where rates are expected to rise, long-duration bonds are more vulnerable.

**Caution / common misconception:** Duration assumes a parallel shift in the yield curve — that all maturities move by the same amount. In reality, short-term rates and long-term rates can move differently (the yield curve can "steepen" or "flatten"). Modified duration and convexity are refinements, but for everyday understanding, duration is the essential tool.

### Concept Spotlight: Strategic Autonomy (World Affairs)

**What it is in plain words:** A country's ability to act independently in foreign policy without being unduly constrained by alliances, dependence on other nations, or external pressure. It is the opposite of "client state" or "vassal." A truly autonomous state can make war, peace, trade deals, and diplomatic moves based purely on its own interests, not out of obligation to a patron.

**Why it exists / what problem it solves:** In a world of great powers (US, China, Russia, EU), smaller countries face a constant trade-off between security and sovereignty. Joining an alliance (NATO, for example) provides protection but limits freedom of action. Strategic autonomy is the ideal goal for countries that want the benefits of cooperation without the constraints.

**Concrete example from today's news:** France and the EU have long advocated for "strategic autonomy" from the US, particularly in energy and defense. As the EU heads into a G20 energy showdown with Trump, Russia, and Venezuela (source 1), its ability to pursue an independent energy policy — separate from US sanctions and Russian supply — is a test of its strategic autonomy. Similarly, India under Modi has cultivated relationships with both the US and Russia/China to maximize its own freedom of action.

**Rough benchmarks worth remembering:** The EU's strategic autonomy is measured by its ability to deploy military force without US logistics (very limited today), its control over key technology supply chains (limited), and its energy independence (improving but not complete). China's strategic autonomy is high because it is nearly self-sufficient in energy, food, and many materials, but it depends on Taiwan for advanced chips.

**Caution / common misconception:** Strategic autonomy does not mean autarky (complete self-sufficiency). No country is fully autonomous. Even the US relies on rare earths from China. The concept is about relative freedom, not absolute. Also, pushing too hard for autonomy can backfire by alienating allies and triggering a hostile response.

### Terms from Today's News

- **Discount rate (in investing context):** The rate used to calculate the present value of future cash flows. When the Fed raises interest rates, the discount rate for all future earnings rises, lowering the current value of stocks, especially those with profits expected far in the future (like AI companies). Today's 10-year yield at 5% is the benchmark discount rate for the US economy.

- **Islanded data center:** A data center that is not connected to the main electrical grid. It generates its own power (often with natural gas generators or dedicated solar/wind) and is "islanded" from the broader network. This is becoming more common as grid interconnection delays lengthen and power demand from AI grows faster than utilities can serve it (source 95).

- **Frozen conflict:** A war that has ended in a ceasefire but not a peace treaty, leaving the underlying dispute unresolved with low levels of violence. Examples: Ukraine in some regions, Korea, Nagorno-Karabakh. Zelensky's humanitarian worker warned this is a real risk for Ukraine (source 57). Frozen conflicts are dangerous because they can re-escalate at any moment.

- **Clarity Act:** Proposed US legislation to create a clear federal regulatory framework for cryptocurrencies. The name "Clarity Act" is meant to contrast with the current state of regulatory uncertainty. The Senate vote is causing anxiety because it includes provisions that would restrict Trump family investments in crypto — a rare intersection of personal financial interest and public policy (source 21).

- **NIMBY (Not In My Back Yard):** A term for local opposition to new developments (data centers, wind farms, housing, etc.) by residents who live nearby. Even when people support renewable energy or AI infrastructure in principle, they often oppose it being built near their homes. This is a major barrier to data center construction (source 15).

- **Abliterated model:** A modified version of an AI model where the safety guardrails (refusal mechanisms, content filters) have been removed. This allows the model to generate any content without restriction, including harmful or unethical outputs. The term comes from "ablate" — to remove a component. Abliterated versions of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash are circulating on Hugging Face (source 104).

- **Yttrium:** A rare-earth element used in lasers, superconductors, and defense electronics. China is the dominant producer (roughly 80% of global supply). By restricting yttrium exports, China can pressure US and other customers ahead of negotiations — a classic example of using resource leverage for geopolitical goals (source 111).
