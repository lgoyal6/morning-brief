**Who wins/loses:** Oil importers (India, Japan, South Korea, Europe) lose if Hormuz gets disrupted. US oil producers (and their investors) win if global supply tightens. Iran's economy gets squeezed further, but the regime has shown it can endure. China, as Iran's biggest oil customer, is caught between buying cheap Iranian crude and angering Washington.

For commodities: it can disrupt palm oil, rubber, cocoa, coffee, and sugar production. For energy: it can reduce hydroelectric power in drought-hit regions. For inflation: food price spikes are a common consequence.

## 2. Markets, Money & Deals

**Bessent says US budget deficit "has likely peaked" — but markets are sceptical**

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said there's a "very good chance" the US budget deficit has peaked under President Trump ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/20/bessent-budget-deficit-trump.html)). However, CNBC also reports that Bessent's efforts to stabilise the Treasury market "so far haven't worked," with experts sceptical he can overcome the forces pushing yields higher ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/20/bessents-efforts-in-the-treasury-market-so-far-havent-worked-heres-what-else-he-can-try.html)).

**Background:** The US budget deficit is the gap between what the government spends and what it collects in taxes. It's currently running at about $2 trillion per year. When the government borrows to cover this gap, it issues Treasury bonds — and the more it borrows, the more supply hits the market, pushing bond prices down and yields (interest rates) up. Bessent has been trying various tactics to manage this, but yields have continued rising.

**Why it matters:** Higher Treasury yields mean higher borrowing costs for everyone — the government, corporations, and homeowners with mortgages. It also makes stocks less attractive (why take risk in stocks when you can get 4-5% "risk-free" from Treasuries?). For the AI buildout, higher rates make it more expensive to finance data centres.

**Memory stocks: the AI boom's darlings explained**

CNBC published a useful explainer on the four major memory stocks benefiting from the AI boom: Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron (MU), and SanDisk (SNDK) ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/21/4-memory-stocks-are-darlings-of-the-ai-boom-how-they-differ-and-why-we-own-micron.html)). While they often trade together, they are not the same:

- **SK Hynix** is the leader in HBM (High-Bandwidth Memory — the specialised memory that sits next to Nvidia's GPUs). It has the most direct AI exposure.
- **Samsung** is the largest overall memory maker but has been slower to ramp HBM. It's also a foundry competitor to TSMC.
- **Micron (MU)** is the US-based memory maker. It's catching up in HBM and benefits from "friendshoring" — US customers wanting a non-Asian supplier.
- **SanDisk (SNDK)** focuses on NAND flash (storage memory, not the HBM used in AI training). It's less directly AI-exposed.

**Why it matters:** Memory is a cyclical business — prices boom and bust. The AI boom has created a super-cycle where HBM demand is structurally growing. Both SK Hynix and Samsung are returning massive cash to shareholders through buybacks ([MarketWatch](https://www.marketwatch.com/story/when-it-comes-to-returning-cash-to-shareholders-anything-sk-hynix-can-do-samsung-can-do-bigger-87db6ef4?mod=mw_rss_topstories)).

## 9. Foundations & Terms

### Concept Spotlight: Bond, Coupon and Yield

**What they are in plain words:**

A **bond** is an IOU. When you buy a bond, you are lending money to a government or company. They promise to pay you back the full amount (the "face value" or "principal") on a specific date (the "maturity date"), and in the meantime, they pay you interest at a fixed rate (the "coupon").

The **coupon** is the annual interest payment, expressed as a percentage of the face value. If a bond has a face value of $1,000 and a 5% coupon, it pays you $50 per year.

The **yield** is the actual return you earn if you buy the bond at its current market price. This is the key concept: **price and yield move in opposite directions.**

**Why they exist / what problem they solve:**

Governments and companies need to borrow money to operate — to build roads, fund wars, expand factories, etc. Bonds are the mechanism for that borrowing. They allow investors to earn a return while taking on a specific level of risk (government bonds are safer, corporate bonds are riskier).

**Why price and yield move opposite — the worked example:**

Imagine a bond with:
- Face value: $1,000
- Coupon: 5% ($50 per year)
- Maturity: 10 years

**Scenario 1:** You buy the bond at issuance for $1,000. Your yield is exactly 5% — the same as the coupon. You get $50/year for 10 years, then your $1,000 back.

**Scenario 2:** Interest rates in the economy rise. New bonds are now being issued with a 6% coupon. Your old bond only pays 5%. Why would anyone buy your bond for $1,000 when they can get 6% elsewhere? They won't. So the price of your bond falls — say to $900. Now, a buyer who pays $900 gets $50/year, which is a 5.56% yield ($50 ÷ $900). The yield has risen because the price fell.

**Scenario 3:** Interest rates fall. New bonds pay 4%. Your 5% bond is now more attractive. People will pay more than $1,000 to get that higher coupon. The price rises to, say, $1,100. The yield falls to 4.55% ($50 ÷ $1,100).

**The rule:** When bond prices go UP, yields go DOWN. When bond prices go DOWN, yields go UP.

**How it shows up in the news:**

When you read "Treasury yields rose today," it means bond prices fell. This usually happens because:
- The economy is strong (investors sell safe bonds to buy risky stocks)
- Inflation is high (investors demand higher yields to compensate for inflation eating their returns)
- The government is issuing lots of new bonds (more supply = lower prices = higher yields)

When you read "yields fell," it means bond prices rose. This happens when:
- Investors are scared (they buy safe bonds as a "flight to safety")
- The Fed cuts rates (new bonds will pay less, so existing bonds become more valuable)
- The economy is weak (investors expect lower rates ahead)

**Rough benchmarks to remember:**
- The 10-year US Treasury yield is the most-watched bond yield in the world. It's the benchmark for everything from mortgage rates to corporate borrowing costs.
- A "normal" 10-year yield is roughly 2-4%. In 2020 it was below 1%. In 2023 it hit 5%. Currently it's around 4-4.5%.
- When the 10-year yield rises above 5%, it usually causes stock market stress because stocks become less attractive compared to "risk-free" bonds.

**Caution / common misconception:**
Many beginners think "yield" is the same as "coupon." It's not. The coupon is fixed — it never changes. The yield changes constantly as the bond's price changes in the market. When you hear "the yield on the 10-year Treasury rose to 4.5%," that's the yield, not the coupon.

### Concept Spotlight: Freedom of Navigation

**What it is in plain words:**

Freedom of Navigation (FON) is a principle of international law that says ships of all countries have the right to sail through the world's oceans without interference. This includes passing through "territorial seas" (the 12-mile zone near a country's coast) as long as the passage is "innocent" (not threatening) and through "straits used for international navigation" (like Hormuz, Malacca, Bab el-Mandeb).

**Why it exists / what problem it solves:**

Without FON, every coastal country could claim huge swaths of ocean and charge tolls or block ships they don't like. Global trade — which moves mostly by ship — would grind to a halt. FON is the legal framework that keeps the ocean's "highways" open. It's codified in the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), which most countries have signed.

**Concrete example from today's news:**

The US military is escorting oil tankers through the Strait of Hormuz ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/21/strait-hormuz-central-command-centcom-oil-iran-war.html)). Iran has threatened to block the strait. The US position is that Hormuz is an "international strait" through which FON must be maintained. The US Navy's presence is not just about protecting oil — it's about enforcing the legal principle that no single country can close a global waterway.

**How it shows up in the news:**

FON disputes happen most often in:
- **The South China Sea:** China claims almost the entire sea as its territory and has built military bases on artificial islands. The US sails warships through the area to assert FON — these are called "FONOPs" (Freedom of Navigation Operations).
- **The Strait of Hormuz:** Iran periodically threatens to block it. The US responds by sending warships through.
- **The Taiwan Strait:** China considers it territorial waters; the US considers it international waters and sails through regularly.

**Rough benchmarks worth remembering:**
- About 90% of global trade moves by ship.
- Three straits — Hormuz, Malacca, and Bab el-Mandeb — are the most critical chokepoints. Disruption in any one of them affects global supply chains.
- The US conducts roughly 30-40 FONOPs per year, mostly in the South China Sea.

**Caution / common misconception:**
FON does NOT mean the US can do whatever it wants. "Innocent passage" means a ship must pass through continuously and expeditiously, not stop, not conduct military exercises, not threaten the coastal state. Warships are allowed but must follow specific rules. The disputes are usually about what counts as "innocent" and whether certain waters are "international" or "territorial."

**Other Terms from Today's News:**

- **Secondary sanctions:** Sanctions that punish third parties (companies, countries) for doing business with a sanctioned country. Example: the US threatens "economic consequences" for nations helping Iran. This is more aggressive than primary sanctions (which only target the country itself) because it forces other countries to choose sides.

- **Foundry (in semiconductors):** A factory that manufactures chips designed by other companies. TSMC is the world's largest foundry — it makes chips for Nvidia, Apple, AMD, and others. Samsung also operates a foundry. When Samsung raises foundry prices by 15%, it means chip designers pay more to have their chips made.

- **HBM (High-Bandwidth Memory):** A type of memory that sits very close to the GPU (processor) in AI servers. It's faster and more power-efficient than regular DRAM. HBM is the most supply-constrained component in AI hardware right now — there isn't enough of it to meet demand.

- **LNG (Liquefied Natural Gas):** Natural gas that has been cooled to liquid form (-162°C) so it can be shipped on tankers, rather than through pipelines. LNg is how gas travels across oceans. Europe's increased LNg imports from the US and Qatar are what replaced Russian pipeline gas after 2022.

- **El Niño:** A natural climate pattern where Pacific Ocean waters near the equator warm more than usual. This shifts weather patterns worldwide — causing droughts in some places, floods in others. It typically lasts 9-12 months. The current El Niño is forecast to be the strongest on record.

- **Ebola:** A viral hemorrhagic fever with a ~50% fatality rate. It spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids (not airborne). Outbreaks are usually contained through contact tracing and isolation, but the current DR Congo outbreak is spreading faster than any on record.
