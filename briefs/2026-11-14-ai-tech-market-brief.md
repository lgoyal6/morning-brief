## 5. Foundations & Terms

### Concept Spotlight: Summit Diplomacy

**What it is:** Summit diplomacy is direct, high-level negotiation between heads of state or government. It is the highest form of diplomacy, bypassing the usual channels of ambassadors and foreign ministers.

**Why it exists:** To break deadlocks, build personal relationships, and make big strategic decisions that lower-level diplomats cannot. A phone call or a handshake between leaders can change the course of history in a way a memo cannot.

**A worked example with real numbers:** The upcoming Trump-Putin-Xi meeting at APEC. The stated goal is "reduction of nuclear weapons." The real work will be on Ukraine, Taiwan, and trade. The meeting is unprecedented because the three largest nuclear powers have not sat down together since the Cold War.

**How it shows up in the news:** "Historic joint meeting," "sidelines of the summit," "breakthrough," "stalemate," "leaders agree to meet."

**A figure worth remembering:** The Yalta Conference in 1945, where Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin redrew the map of Europe. It is the classic example of summit diplomacy shaping the post-war world.

**A common misconception:** That the public handshake or joint press conference is the substance. The real work happens in private bilaterals and working dinners. The public event is often theater designed to signal a shift in relations.

- **Concentration Risk:** The risk of loss because too much of your portfolio (or a system) is tied up in a single asset, sector, or provider. Why it exists: If you put all your money in one stock, or if the entire world runs on one cloud provider, a problem with that single entity causes a systemic failure. How it shows up: The Microsoft outage is a concentration risk event. The AI trade being dominated by Nvidia is a concentration risk for investors. Concrete example: An investor who only owns NVDA stock. If Nvidia has a scandal, their entire portfolio crashes. Diversification is the solution.

- **Digital Sovereignty:** The concept that a nation has the right and responsibility to control its own digital infrastructure, data, and technology supply chains, rather than relying on foreign providers. Why it exists: The Microsoft outage and FAA ground stop are perfect examples of what happens when critical infrastructure is concentrated in a few foreign hands. How it shows up: Calls for "European cloud," "Indian AI models," "domestic chip fabrication." Rough numbers: India's "IndiaAI" mission is a $1.2 billion sovereign AI play.
