## 8. Terms & Concepts

**Concept Spotlight: Free Cash Flow (FCF)**

- **What it is:** Free Cash Flow is the cash a company generates after accounting for capital expenditures (capex) needed to maintain or expand its asset base. The formula: **Operating Cash Flow – Capex = FCF**. It's a measure of how much "extra" cash a company has to pay dividends, buy back stock, reduce debt, or invest in new projects.
- **Concrete example:** Amazon reported operating cash flow of $X and capex of $Y in its Q2 earnings. If operating cash flow is $100B and capex is $80B, FCF = $20B. If capex jumps to $120B, FCF becomes negative ($-20B) — meaning the company is spending more than it generates from operations.
- **Real use case for investors:** FCF is a more reliable indicator of financial health than net income, because net income includes non-cash items (depreciation, amortization, stock-based compensation). For capital-intensive industries like AI infrastructure (hyperscalers, semiconductor fabs, data centers), FCF is the key metric to watch. If a company boosts capex but FCF stays positive, the growth is self-funded. If FCF turns negative, the company is burning cash. In the previous brief, Alphabet went cash-flow negative for the first time — that's a red flag for investors.
- **Rough numbers/benchmarks:** A healthy FCF yield (FCF / Market Cap) is typically 4–6% for mature companies. For fast-growing tech, a negative FCF is common and acceptable if revenue growth is accelerating. But negative FCF combined with slowing growth is a danger signal.
- **Caution/common misconception:** Many investors confuse FCF with net income. A company can report positive net income but negative FCF if it's spending heavily on capex or building inventory. Also, stock-based compensation is a non-cash expense that reduces net income but doesn't directly affect FCF — but it does dilute shareholders. Always look at FCF per share, not just absolute FCF.

**Other Terms:**

Higher credit quality means lower risk of default. In the context of AI infrastructure, credit quality matters for data center REITs (like DLR, EQIX) and utility companies building power infrastructure — they need to borrow at low rates to fund capex.

- **Credit Spreads:** The difference in yield between a corporate bond and a risk-free government bond (like a U.S. Treasury) of the same maturity. It reflects the market's perception of credit risk. Widening spreads mean investors are demanding higher compensation for risk, which is a negative signal for the economy. Narrowing spreads indicate confidence. In the current environment, oil prices and geopolitical risk could push credit spreads wider.

- **Operating Expense (Opex):** The day-to-day costs a company incurs to run its business — salaries, rent, utilities, marketing, R&D. It's distinct from **capital expenditure (capex)**, which is investment in long-term assets like buildings, equipment, and data centers. For AI companies, opex includes cloud computing costs, model training, and employee salaries. A company trying to improve profitability will focus on reducing opex relative to revenue.

- **LEO (Low Earth Orbit):** The region of space up to about 2,000 km above Earth's surface. Satellites in LEO are used for communications (Starlink), Earth observation, and navigation. LEO is important for AI infrastructure because satellite internet provides connectivity for remote data centers and edge computing.
