The takeaway: the "chip war" (export controls, tariffs, investment restrictions designed to slow China's advanced semiconductor industry) stays fully intact. The hotline is a safety valve so that a miscalculation during a crisis does not escalate into direct conflict, not a step toward detente.

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate by a quarter point on September 16 to a target range of 3.75% to 4.00%, its first hike since 2023, aimed at cooling inflation that has stayed above the 2% target ([eztaxreturn.com](https://www.eztaxreturn.com/blog/how-does-a-fed-interest-rate-hike-affect-you)). A "hawk" (someone who prioritizes fighting inflation over supporting growth) wanting higher rates tells you the Fed's internal debate is about whether one more hike is needed, not about cutting.

The consequence is visible in the bond market. The 10-year Treasury yield (the interest rate the US government pays on its 10-year bonds, and the benchmark for borrowing costs across the economy) has climbed to a 19-year high. Three forces are pushing it: sticky inflation, heavy bond issuance as the government borrows more, and an AI-fueled investment boom that is sucking up capital ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/26/10-year-treasury-yield-is-at-its-highest-in-19-years-how-we-got-here.html)).

This creates a problem for the Fed. Normally high yields do the Fed's work for it by cooling the economy. But these yields are partly a symptom of massive private investment (data centers) and heavy government borrowing, not just inflation fears. So high yields and high inflation coexist, and the Fed is squeezed: it wants to fight inflation but knows a rate hike could break the very investment boom driving growth ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/09/24/surging-treasury-yields-are-posing-a-brand-new-problem-for-kevin-warsh-and-the-fed.html)).

### Goldman's $7.6 trillion AI spending forecast, and the shift beyond GPUs

Goldman Sachs projects that hyperscalers (the giant cloud companies: Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta) will spend $1.2 trillion on data centers and related long-lived assets (capex) in 2027 and $1.4 trillion in 2028, part of a cumulative $7.6 trillion AI boom ([247wallst.com](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/09/26/goldman-sachs-says-a-7-6-trillion-ai-spending-boom-is-coming-skip-the-gpus-follow-the-money-here)). The intriguing argument: the first phase of the AI boom rewarded the companies selling processors (Nvidia, AMD). The next phase may reward the companies supplying everything needed to keep those processors running: power, cooling, networking, land, construction.

This is why the "AI infrastructure" lens is broader than chips. The binding constraint on the buildout is increasingly power, not silicon. That explains why Google's "Suncatcher" project is looking at space-based data centers, with the site race extending from the Arctic and the ocean to orbit ([finance.biggo.com](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMidkFVX3lxTE9CajR2UkVHaG5BRjM5SnRpWUoxWFhNR21KTzhJYWludHIxdDR4bmZYaTJDV1JXdzFaZ0FORFAwYmh6am5tcUloOG5Pajh0OUtzUHpRcjNPb3k3UXdHVzcwREhMTTZaMFRHXzB3N2hKZEQtT3VuUGc?oc=5)). And it explains why the blue-collar AI job market is booming: HVAC, plumbing, welding, and electrical workers are in demand to build these facilities, even as public backlash slows development in some states ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/26/blue-collar-jobs-ai-data-center-backlash.html)).

### The Anthropic-Akamai record deal and edge inference

Anthropic signed an $11.6 billion deal with Akamai, a content-delivery network (CDN, the infrastructure that caches and serves web content close to users), setting a record for the partnership ([The Cryptonomist](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMidEFVX3lxTFAtWDhkZjBFbWM1Y2ZMWnhYajE4czJ1bVVhREFkTzBrLVRRWkRyc1ZDaktXN25JcWNWNVI5SExmazlWTXFjWjBjdTgwVGNLa0ZKS1R2dXBiUF84OFZfczMzQkFmaU90aHMzWWVLcW5HZXBWNHFu?oc=5)). For the buildout, it means the total computing footprint is larger than just the big core data centers; it spreads into the network itself.

### TSMC: the central beneficiary, and still "undervalued"?

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSM) delivered 33.7% year-over-year revenue growth, a 67.7% gross margin, and a 60.3% operating margin in Q2 2026, and is guiding for 37% year-over-year revenue growth in Q3. On Seeking Alpha's numbers, it trades at 20.37 times 2027 expected earnings, with earnings per share projected to grow nearly 30% annually ([Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4949681-taiwan-semiconductor-ai-capex-keeps-climbing-and-tsmc-looks-undervalued)). Recall the two margins: gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of making the chips; operating margin further subtracts R&D and sales costs. Both being over 60% is extraordinary for a manufacturer and reflects TSMC's monopoly-like position in advanced chips. Whether 20x forward earnings is "cheap" depends on whether the 30% growth materializes, which depends on the capex boom persisting.

The British AI "neocloud" (a data-center company built specifically to rent out AI compute, as opposed to a general-purpose cloud like AWS) Nscale secured $3.36 billion in convertible financing from investors including Third Point and Nvidia (NVDA), ahead of a potential US IPO ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/25/ahead-of-u-s-ipo-british-ai-neocloud-nscale-secures-3-36b-in-convertible-finacing/)). ## 4. Model & Research Watch

### Xiaomi MiMo-V2.6: cheap, open-weight, and the price war accelerates

Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6 topped a global open-weights AI leaderboard and was reportedly built for roughly $2.62 million in training cost. The company prices it at about a fifth of what Anthropic charges for comparable capability ([startupfortune.com](https://startupfortune.com/xiaomi-prices-its-top-ai-model-at-a-fifth-of-what-anthropic-charges)). The release came less than two weeks after Anthropic accused Xiaomi of routing over 400,000 conversations through its Claude model to help train MiMo, naming Xiaomi among seven Chinese labs in a "distillation" threat report (distillation being when you train a smaller model on the outputs of a larger one, a shortcut to capability).

Two implications. First, open-weights models with near-frontier quality at a fifth of the price compress margins for every US lab selling API access. Second, the cost structure is starkly different: training for millions, not billions, because Chinese labs are optimizing aggressively for efficiency, partly because export controls cut them off from the best US chips. The Victorians would call this "necessity as the mother of invention."

## 8. Building Your Knowledge

- **Real vs nominal yields are the difference between "rates are high" and "lenders are getting richer."** The 10-year Treasury at a 19-year high tells you nominal yield, the sticker price. What matters for the economy is the real yield: nominal minus expected inflation. If nominal is 4.5% and inflation is 3%, the real yield is 1.5%. When real yields rise, the cost of borrowing for everyone rises. See Concept Spotlight below for the full mechanism.

- **The discount rate is the master gauge for long-duration assets.** Data centers, chip fabs, and streaming libraries all generate cash far in the future. When the discount rate (driven by Treasury yields) rises, those future cash flows are worth less today. This is why a rising 10-year yield hits TSMC and Amazon harder than a company with near-term earnings like Costco or Coca-Cola. The single most useful habit for a young investor: when you see a yield spike, ask "what long-dated asset is being repriced today?"

- **"Spectacle over substance" is a category of diplomacy, not a failure of it.** The Trump-Xi summit was heavy on pomp and light on deliverables ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/25/trump-xi-summit-takeaways.html)). ## 9. Foundations & Terms

### Concept Spotlight: Real Interest Rates

**What it is in plain words:** The nominal interest rate (what you see quoted, like a 10-year Treasury at 4%) minus expected inflation. It is the "true" cost of borrowing after accounting for the fact that inflation erodes the value of the money you repay. If you lend at 4% and inflation is 3%, you are really earning 1% in purchasing power.

**Why it exists:** Borrowers ask "what will this loan cost me in real terms?" Lenders ask "what will I actually be able to buy with the repayments?" Inflation is the hidden tax that erodes both. The real rate is the honest number both parties should care about.

**A concrete worked example:** Say a 10-year Treasury yields 4.5% and the market expects inflation to average 2.5% over the decade. The real yield is 2.0%. Now the Fed raises rates and the nominal yield goes to 5.0%, but inflation expectations also rise to 3.5%. The real yield is now 1.5%, actually lower, meaning borrowing just got cheaper in real terms even though the nominal rate is higher. The confusion between nominal and real is why "rates are high" headlines often mislead.

**How it shows up in the news today:** The 10-year Treasury is at a 19-year high ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/26/10-year-treasury-yield-is-at-its-highest-in-19-years-how-we-got-here.html)). The Fed has hiked to 3.75-4% with sticky inflation ([eztaxreturn.com](https://www.eztaxreturn.com/blog/how-does-a-fed-interest-rate-hike-affect-you)). If inflation expectations stay elevated, the real yield may not have risen as much as the nominal headline suggests, which is why long-dated tech stocks have not collapsed entirely.

**Rough benchmarks worth remembering:** Historically, a 10-year real yield near 0% or negative means borrowing is nearly free in real terms (a boom stimulus); near 2% it is historically normal-ish; above 2.5-3% it is restrictive and tends to crack leverage. The golden rule: when real yields are high and rising, long-duration assets (growth stocks, real estate, crypto) are under pressure.

**Caution or common misconception:** The real yield is not directly observable. It is a market-derived estimate based on the spread between regular Treasuries and inflation-protected bonds (TIPS), and it embeds the market's guess about future inflation, which can be wrong. When inflation surprises to the upside, the realized real yield turns out lower than expected, which is why lenders demand a cushion.

### Concept Spotlight: Extended Deterrence (the nuclear umbrella)

**What it is in plain words:** When one country promises to treat an attack on an ally as an attack on itself, including the possible use of its nuclear weapons, that promise is called extended deterrence. The US "nuclear umbrella" covers NATO allies, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan in practice, even though the wording differs.

**Why it exists:** The purpose is to prevent war by making the cost of attacking an ally higher than any possible benefit. If a rival knows that invading a small ally triggers a response from a nuclear superpower, the invasion looks much less attractive. The US extends deterrence because keeping allies safe is a geopolitical asset: allies host US bases, share intelligence, and buy US weapons.

**A concrete worked example:** Taiwan has no formal US defense treaty, but the US has long signaled that a Chinese invasion would trigger a massive US response, potentially including nuclear force. The credibility of that promise is what the "arming Taiwan" debate is really about ([The Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/international/article-909758)). When the US sells Taiwan weapons, it is not just providing hardware; it is sending a signal of commitment, a bond that increases the credibility of the extended deterrent. The hard part: a promise to risk San Francisco or Seattle for Taipei is only credible if the US actually believes Taiwan is worth it. Extended deterrence is a confidence game where the signal matters as much as the hardware.

**Rough benchmarks worth remembering:** Extended deterrence is considered more credible when (1) the defender has troops deployed in the ally's territory (forward presence), (2) there is a legal treaty, and (3) the defender's own economy is entangled with the ally's. Taiwan scores low on all three, which is exactly why the US is so anxious about signaling commitment.

**Caution or common misconception:** Extended deterrence can fail, and the credible threat of escalation can backfire into a spiral. The NATO Article 5 commitment is the most explicitly codified version (an attack on one member is an attack on all), yet its credibility is constantly debated precisely because nobody can be sure a nation will sacrifice its own cities for another's. Deterrence is a belief held in the attacker's mind, not a physical shield, and it is only as strong as the defender's demonstrated willingness to follow through.

### Terms from Today's News

- **Convertible financing:** Debt that the lender can choose to convert into shares of the company later, at a preset price or formula. Used when a company needs cash now but wants to delay setting a firm share price, as with Nscale's $3.36 billion raise ahead of its IPO ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/25/ahead-of-u-s-ipo-british-ai-neocloud-nscale-secures-3-36b-in-convertible-finacing/)). For the company, it avoids immediate dilution (decreasing existing shareholders' ownership stake), but it creates future dilution when the debt converts.

- **Pruning (in AI):** A compression technique where you train a large model, then methodically remove the least important connections or parameters, shrinking the model to run on less hardware while keeping most of its capability. Aikido's Altar-1 was pruned down to 328 GB ([MarkTechPost](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMizgFBVV95cUxNS3daWVZmWTRDVmxmaVYyNmVfUW9NajI2aE1MdUxkdmRUMUp5bGgyZGpnaHVwTktQcnhFanB6VEctTC1aSmNvOU1sZF96LTcwUl80T3pBdm9tNF9DbnNRNzhwMzNfNV9ENjhfdkFyS1FtN3B4aEowSHltX3lLTnByWjJ6UWdGQXpZcnc4ZWdzMnFLVG94OEtrdlJhVzg0d1R0NVhNci1BYndYMGw5YnZuWEJQZHR5RE9MdjBEUTBTSU1HakVhMl9YTDRqQ3NZUdIB0wFBVV95cUxPdktrSkNxWFBjNWhaTENsSllWMGpVbjVFY1dNZGtSaTV0elF3Ti1MeUxMeklJcEtWMUJwUjltRE9yNmxVRG5tbG9iSnNqcjBJT181QzFTTGNSZVV1ZTgwYXlITGF2eTBMX0hGd2ZxNTRVbjc3aW9yU0lzRUQ3MUhkb1dWazZnRWJwcTNYRTdEMkRVODEybFQ5VE5wcWhqQVZGMXpUZ3hOWmU5N1BHZVpGMEExWGE2TGp2QnRKTzdPbE9TRTJLaWFSVDJGN3BRZkNORVpz?oc=5)).

- **Gross margin vs operating margin:** Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of making the product (for TSMC, the silicon), expressed as a percent. Operating margin subtracts everything else needed to run the business: R&D, sales, administration. TSMC's 67.7% gross and 60.3% operating margins are both remarkable ([Seeking Alpha](https://seekingalpha.com/article/4949681-taiwan-semiconductor-ai-capex-keeps-climbing-and-tsmc-looks-undervalued)). The gap between the two shows how much a company is spending on future growth and overhead.

- **Stablecoin:** A crypto token designed to hold a fixed value, usually $1, backed by reserves of real assets (like dollars or Treasuries). The Fed is now proposing a regulatory framework for payment stablecoins under the GENIUS Act ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/bcreg20260924a.htm)). The question regulators are answering: if a stablecoin is used like a bank deposit, what rules protect the holder?

- **Neocloud:** A data-center company built specifically to rent out AI compute (GPU capacity), rather than a general-purpose cloud like AWS or Azure. Nscale is a British neocloud raising money to build AI data centers ahead of an IPO ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/25/ahead-of-u-s-ipo-british-ai-neocloud-nscale-secures-3-36b-in-convertible-finacing/)). Neoclouds are the pure-play bet on AI infrastructure demand without the distraction of running a general cloud business.
