## 8. Building Your Knowledge

- **The Fed's dilemma in one sentence:** The Fed (the US central bank, which sets the cost of borrowing) wants low inflation and full employment, but it has one main tool (interest rates) for both. When oil spikes push inflation up, the Fed must choose between fighting inflation (raising rates, which slows the economy) and supporting growth (cutting rates, which lets inflation run). This trade-off is the core of every macro story.

- **Tariffs are a tax, and someone always pays.** When Canada puts a 50% tariff on US steel, the US steel exporter doesn't just eat the cost. Either the exporter lowers prices to stay competitive, the Canadian buyer pays more, or the cost passes down the supply chain to consumers. The question "who actually pays" (tariff incidence) is always an empirical one, not a political slogan.

- **Warrants are a bet on future success.** When Amazon gets warrants to buy Qualcomm stock at a set price, Amazon is betting Qualcomm's stock will rise. It's a way to align incentives: Amazon gets upside if Qualcomm's AI chips succeed, and Qualcomm gets a committed customer. It's a common structure in strategic tech deals.

- **The difference between a merger and a reverse merger.** In a normal merger, the bigger company buys the smaller. In a reverse merger, the smaller company technically buys the bigger one, often for regulatory reasons (bank charters, licenses, or brand considerations). The WaFd-EverBank deal is a reverse merger: EverBank is bigger, but WaFd's corporate structure survives.

- **"Sovereign AI" is the new industrial policy.** Countries are realizing that AI is strategic infrastructure, like electricity or railroads were in earlier centuries. Mistral's €3 billion raise is Europe's answer to US and Chinese AI dominance. Expect more government-backed AI funding, and expect it to be framed in national security terms.

- **The power gap is the AI trade's physical limit.** You can't train or run AI models without electricity. Every data center announcement is also a power announcement. The companies that control power (utilities, nuclear operators) have pricing power that chipmakers don't, because power is harder to substitute than a GPU.

## 9. Foundations & Terms

### Concept Spotlight: Warrants (Markets/Investing)

**What it is in plain words:** A warrant is a financial instrument that gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy a company's stock at a fixed price before a certain date. Think of it as a coupon that says "you can buy this stock at $X anytime in the next 5 years."

**Why it exists:** Companies issue warrants to sweeten deals. Instead of paying cash, they offer the counterparty a chance to profit if the stock rises. It aligns incentives: the warrant holder wants the company to succeed, because that makes the warrant valuable.

**A concrete worked example:** Amazon gets warrants to buy Qualcomm stock at, say, $150 per share, exercisable over 5 years. If Qualcomm's stock rises to $200, Amazon can exercise the warrant, buy at $150, and immediately have a $50 per share gain. If Qualcomm's stock falls to $100, Amazon simply doesn't exercise; the warrant expires worthless. Amazon's downside is limited to the price it paid for the warrants (if anything), while its upside is the stock's appreciation above the strike price.

**How it shows up in the news:** The Qualcomm-Amazon deal is a textbook case ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/08/qualcomm-amazon-data-center-infrastructure-deal.html)). Amazon is getting $4 billion worth of warrants as part of an AI infrastructure deal. It's a way for Amazon to participate in Qualcomm's upside without buying the whole company.

**Rough benchmarks:** Warrants typically have longer durations (years) than options (months). They're often issued at a slight premium to the current stock price. They dilute existing shareholders when exercised, because new shares are created.

**Caution:** Warrants are not the same as options, though they work similarly. Options are standardized and traded on exchanges; warrants are issued by the company itself and often have longer durations. Also, warrants can expire worthless, so they're not free money.

### Concept Spotlight: Tariff Incidence (World Affairs)

**What it is in plain words:** Tariff incidence is the question of who actually pays for a tariff (a tax on imported goods). The politician who imposes the tariff says "the foreign country pays." The reality is usually more complicated: the cost is shared between the foreign exporter (who may lower prices to stay competitive) and the domestic importer or consumer (who pays higher prices).

**Why it exists:** Tariffs are a policy tool, but their effects are economic. Understanding incidence matters because it determines who is hurt and who benefits. If the foreign exporter absorbs the cost, the tariff is a successful pressure tool. If domestic consumers absorb it, the tariff is a tax on your own people.

**A concrete worked example:** Canada puts a 50% tariff on US steel. A US steel company sells at $100 per ton. With the tariff, the Canadian buyer must pay $150 per ton unless the US company lowers its price. The US company might cut its price to $80, meaning it earns less but keeps the Canadian customer. The Canadian buyer pays $120 ($80 + $40 tariff). The cost is shared: the US company loses $20 per ton, the Canadian buyer pays $20 more, and the Canadian government collects $40 in tariff revenue.

**How it shows up in the news:** The Canada-US tariff war is a live example ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/08/canada-retaliatory-tariffs.html)). Canada's 50% tariffs on US steel and aluminum will hit US exporters, but the question is how much they absorb versus pass on.

**Rough benchmarks:** Studies of the 2018 US-China trade war found that US consumers and importers absorbed nearly all of the tariff cost, meaning the tariffs functioned as a tax on US consumers rather than on China.
