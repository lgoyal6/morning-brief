## 5. Foundations & Terms

### Concept Spotlight: Merger Arbitrage
**What it is:** Merger arbitrage (or "arb") is a trading strategy that aims to profit from the completion of a merger or acquisition. When Company A announces it will buy Company B for $X per share, Company B's stock usually jumps but trades at a slight discount to $X. The difference is the "spread." An arbitrageur buys Company B's stock, betting the deal will close, and captures that spread.
**Why it exists:** The spread exists because there is risk. The deal might fall through due to antitrust concerns, shareholder rejection, or financing issues. The arb provides liquidity and helps ensure the market prices the deal correctly.
**Worked example with real numbers:** Sanofi offered $175 per share for Insmed. If Insmed trades at $170, the spread is $5. If the deal closes in 6 months, the annualized return is roughly 6% ($5 / $170 * 2). If the deal breaks, Insmed's stock might fall back to $100, causing a 41% loss. The arb is a bet on the process, not the company.
**How it shows up in the news:** When a deal is announced, analysts will discuss the "arb spread" and the probability of the deal closing. The Uber/Delivery Hero deal and the Sanofi/Insmed deal are perfect examples.
**Figure worth remembering:** The spread on a typical large-cap deal is often 2-5% for deals expected to close in 3-6 months.
**Common misconception:** Merger arb is not risk-free. It is a form of event-driven investing with specific risks (regulatory, financing, market).

**Terms from today's news:**
- **Take-or-Pay Contract:** A contract that obligates the buyer to pay for a minimum quantity of a good or service, regardless of whether they take delivery. This is how data centers guarantee power supply and how they secure GPU allocations. It shifts volume risk from the seller to the buyer. Example: A data center operator signs a take-or-pay contract for 100 MW of power. Even if they only use 80 MW, they pay for 100 MW. This is why data center operators are so desperate to fill their capacity.
- **Antitrust:** Laws designed to prevent monopolies and promote competition. In the US, the DOJ and FTC enforce them. They can block or challenge mergers (like the Sanofi/Insmed deal or Uber/Delivery Hero deal) if they believe it will harm consumers or create a monopoly. This is the primary risk that creates the spread in merger arbitrage.
- **Guidance:** A company's forward-looking statement about its expected financial performance (revenue, earnings, etc.). Guidance matters more than past results because the stock market prices in the future. When a company "beats" earnings but issues weak guidance, the stock often falls. When it "misses" but raises guidance, it often rises. This is a core concept for earnings season.
- **MoE (Mixture-of-Experts):** A neural network architecture where the model is composed of many smaller "expert" sub-networks and a "router" that decides which experts to use for each input (token). This allows the model to have a very large total number of parameters (knowledge) while keeping the number of active parameters (compute cost) low. Ant Group's LingSante (560B total, 25B active) and Sber's GigaChat 4 (708B total) are MoE models.
