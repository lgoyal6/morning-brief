## 8. Terms & Concepts

### Concept Spotlight: Yield Curve

**What it is:** The yield curve is a graph plotting the interest rates of bonds with different maturities, from short-term (3 months, 2 years) to long-term (10 years, 30 years). Normally, long-term bonds pay higher yields because investors demand compensation for locking up money longer (the "term premium"). An **inverted yield curve** — when short-term rates are higher than long-term rates — is a rare and historically reliable recession signal.

**Concrete example:** As of August 3, 2026, the 2-year U.S. Treasury yields 4.30%, while the 10-year yields 4.61%. That's a *positive* slope (normal). But earlier in the cycle, the 2-year was above the 10-year, creating an inversion. The curve is now "steepening" but still relatively flat by historical standards.

**Real use case for investors:** When the yield curve inverts, it signals that markets expect the Fed to cut rates in the future (because the economy will weaken). That's why short-term rates are high (Fed fighting inflation) but long-term rates are lower (expecting recession). Investors use the spread between 2-year and 10-year yields as a key indicator. A sustained inversion has preceded every U.S. recession since the 1970s.

**Rough numbers/benchmarks:**
- Normal curve: 2-year ~3%, 10-year ~5% (spread of +2%)
- Inverted curve: 2-year ~5%, 10-year ~4% (spread of -1%)
- Current: 2-year 4.30%, 10-year 4.61% (spread +0.31% — barely positive, but recently inverted)

**Caution/common misconception:** The yield curve inversion doesn't predict the *timing* of a recession — it can stay inverted for 1-2 years before a downturn hits. Also, the curve can "un-invert" (steepen) as the economy actually enters recession, because the Fed starts cutting short-term rates. So an inversion is a warning, not a trigger.

### Other Terms

- **Forward Guidance:** Communication from a central bank about its intended future policy path. The Fed's July 2026 statement omitted forward guidance for the first time in a decade, making rate decisions harder to predict and increasing market volatility.
- **Free Cash Flow (FCF):** Operating cash flow minus capital expenditure. It measures the cash a company has left after maintaining its asset base. Google went FCF-negative in Q2 2026 because its AI data center capex ($44.9B) exceeded operating cash flow. Investors watch FCF because it funds dividends, buybacks, and debt repayment.
- **Sovereign AI:** The concept of nations building their own AI capabilities (compute infrastructure, models, data) to avoid dependence on a few U.S. or Chinese companies. Nvidia's "sovereign AI" business is a growing revenue stream, as countries buy GPU clusters for national AI initiatives.
- **Open-weights model:** An AI model whose trained parameters (weights) are released publicly, allowing anyone to download, run, fine-tune, and audit it. Kimi K3 and Qwen3.8-Max are open-weight models. This is different from "open-source" (which also includes training code, data, and methodology). Open-weight models lower barriers to entry but can't be fully reproduced without the training recipe.
- **Agent Benchmarks:** Standardized tests to evaluate how well AI agents (systems that can take actions, browse the web, use tools) perform on real-world tasks. The new OpenClaw and Ollama paper discusses frameworks for evaluating autonomous agents. This is a rapidly evolving field — agent benchmarks are less mature than language model benchmarks.
- **Token efficiency:** A measure of how many tokens (words/pieces of text) a model needs to process a given input. More efficient models use fewer tokens for the same task, reducing compute cost and latency. Kimi K3's MoE architecture achieves high token efficiency by activating only 16 of 896 experts per token.
