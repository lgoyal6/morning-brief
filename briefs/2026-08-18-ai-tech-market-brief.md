## 2. Markets, Money & Deals

**30-year Treasury yield hits 5.33%, a new 19-year high** — The yield on the 30-year US government bond (the cost of borrowing for the US government for 30 years) rose to 5.33%, the highest level since 2007 ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/18/treasury-yields-.html)). This is driven by worsening US fiscal situation (the government is running large deficits) and persistently higher inflation. This matters for EVERYTHING: higher long-term borrowing costs mean higher mortgage rates, higher corporate debt costs, and — critically for the AI buildout — higher financing costs for the huge amounts of debt being used to build data centres. The 10-year yield is also up, but the 30-year is the "canary in the coal mine" for long-term inflation expectations.

**Home Depot beats earnings but reaffirms guidance amid 'frozen housing market'** — Home Depot (HD) reported Q2 results that beat Wall Street expectations on revenue and earnings per share ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/18/home-depot-hd-q2-2026-earnings.html)). However, the company reaffirmed its full-year guidance and described the housing market as "frozen" — meaning that high mortgage rates (driven by the higher Treasury yields above) are crushing home sales, which in turn hurts demand for home improvement goods. This is a classic "beat now but cautious outlook" situation.

**Anthropic's annualized revenue run rate hits $65 billion — up sevenfold from a year ago** — Anthropic, the AI company behind the Claude model family, told investors that its annualized revenue run rate (a projection of what the company would earn in a year if it kept earning at the current monthly rate) climbed to $65 billion in July ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/17/anthropic-says-annualized-revenue-climbed-to-65-billion-in-july.html)). The revenue is coming from enterprise API usage and subscriptions.

## 3. AI & Infrastructure

**Nvidia's AI moat is shifting from chips to capital** — Nvidia still dominates the AI chip market, but with competition from AMD, Intel, and custom chips (ASICs) from companies like Google and Amazon, the company is increasingly using its massive cash pile to build a different kind of moat: financing infrastructure ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/18/nvidias-ai-moat-is-shifting-from-chips-to-capital.html)). Last week, Nvidia and six of the world's largest investment firms signed MOUs (memorandums of understanding — non-binding agreements to explore a deal) to channel more than $500 billion into AI infrastructure, in a structure that lets tech companies build data centres without loading the cost onto their own balance sheets ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/08/17/what-nvidias-500-billion-wall-street-deal-signals-about-the-ai-boom)). Nvidia's strategy: if you can't buy its chips, it will help you find the money to buy them. This is a "virtuous cycle" — Nvidia's massive cash flow (from selling chips) is being reinvested into financing more data centres, which will buy more Nvidia chips.

**AMD claims its 2026 rack-scale AI solution is 4x more energy efficient than its 2024 platform** — AMD announced that its 2026 rack-scale AI system (a complete server rack designed for AI workloads) is 4x more energy efficient than its 2024 platform, and says it's pacing ahead of a 20x efficiency improvement target by 2030 ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/amd-claims-its-2026-rack-scale-ai-solution-is-4x-more-energy-efficient-than-its-2024-ai-platform-company-says-its-pacing-ahead-of-20x-efficiency-by-2030)). However, AMD did not provide actual benchmark results to back up the claim. This is a key competitive battle: Nvidia's dominance is partly due to its power efficiency per FLOP (floating-point operation), and if AMD can close the gap, it could win business from cost-conscious hyperscalers.

**AI data centres are cementing natural gas as the US grid's indispensable fuel** — The surge in data centre electricity demand is making natural gas — not renewables — the primary beneficiary, because gas plants can be built quickly and run 24/7, unlike solar and wind which are intermittent (only produce when the sun shines or wind blows) ([MarketScale](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMivwFBVV95cUxNVVVCREYwazVnelhCQkR0YjZ2MkRSNGhUZDk3REpXOUFwa2VTZHlmOHc5TzZCQWRWZ2c4RjA4MWs0LWxVcGVDRE1Td3hPNUdHMWhrNmwyYzJ0VlZteFk3LUJwTWJDNW5iME9icUVnV25CT1J3cTJDY3FrVUpOdUJDenlOakNFQ25RcW9lSUx5eVlwZE1sbGoyWnFJZFpBdmhUekd4ajRKUGVHZEZzY09uV0pIVVlURmtCSkdIMlQ1NA?oc=5)). This is a major shift from the narrative that AI would accelerate renewable adoption. Instead, the immediate need for reliable, dispatchable power is favouring gas. This has implications for the stocks of natural gas producers, pipeline companies, and gas-fired power plant operators.

**Rubicon Professional Services surpasses 1 GW of battery energy storage capacity** — Rubicon, a battery energy storage company, has crossed 1 GW (gigawatt) of installed capacity, driven by AI data centre demand ([EEJournal](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMi-AFBVV95cUxOSEV3SV85amlhSndNTm1jM1NPWDBDYmcwS25RMUgwU0lySFNlNV9CUmNDTWFNRXJuejNEVk5pelVrcTNZeml2a3hFeGlKa2RiNW5kLTJiS2hjVFhOMnZUQjNYWUlWc1czNWNvaVVzclJ3N3N5aE8zWER2UWp4a2Y5TlI4WWtMSVF6Y1lfSlFmZEtEc0tieUE0TmNLbzNMMFJkQXNOZ2doMW43YnA0cHVJamV1bnhFWDFsWXJmWjc1LXhzQ0VZZUt0V2FjWHNfWUd1MG5qUWRfUGZQeDhkby1US1JtazBEa3FPdTZxcm54TlI4X21VeFN6NA?oc=5)). The intersection of AI, power, and storage is a key investing theme.

**US export controls harm Nvidia, AMD, and Intel beyond just lost sales** — A new analysis argues that export controls (restrictions on selling advanced chips to China and other countries) hurt US chip companies not just by reducing revenue, but by damaging their ability to fund R&D and compete globally ([Forbes](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMitgFBVV95cUxNRkZBLUtlTlowQUd6enBHUnlFWDZvRGtZcnN2UGhRbGNRWmgzaDRJcGNKdHFhUk5UeXpEbGNtYVVSeUdTZGVkbTkwUHE2TjhCa1haNXZrMGI2bDh2dzBVWnU4Ty1tRnBqV2ZxaDJOczU2WVNGZS1WR1ZQOXhSRk9QekZtYzNjS1VWb2Y2SXk4cmdfems3MndJYm1PenNuYmNYOFJ0Z21pNkRBTy1QZDZTeks3MW5oZw?oc=5)). ## 8. Building Your Knowledge

1. **When the 30-year Treasury yield hits a 19-year high, it's not just a "bond market story" — it's the single most important macro signal for every asset you own.** Higher long-term yields raise the bar for stock valuations, especially for growth/tech stocks that promise most of their profits far in the future. The "risk-free rate" is the baseline for all investing. When it rises, something has to give — either stock prices fall to reflect the new discount rate, or earnings must grow faster than expected. Today's selloff (META -7%, ARM -10%) is the market adjusting to that new reality.

2. **The difference between "annualized revenue run rate" and "actual revenue" matters.** Anthropic is telling investors its revenue run rate is $65B — meaning if July's revenue were sustained for 12 months, it would be $65B. But revenue may not be linear: it could be growing or seasonal. The run rate is a forward-looking projection, not a backward-looking fact. It's what companies use to hype investors before an IPO. Always ask: "What was the actual revenue last quarter?"

3. **Export controls are a distinct type of sanction — they restrict technology, not goods.** Normal sanctions block trade in oil, weapons, or luxury goods. Export controls restrict the *knowledge* and *tools* needed to make advanced products. The chip war uses export controls: you can't sell a Chinese company the software to design a 3nm chip, or the machine that makes it. This is harder to enforce than a trade embargo because technology is intangible — it can be downloaded, stolen, or reverse-engineered. The US is trying to restrict the *means of production*, not just the product.

4. **The "AI buildout" is entering a phase where the inputs (power, debt, memory, chips) are more important than the models.** Over the last year, the story was "which model is best?" (Claude vs. GPT vs. Gemini). Now, the story is "how do we get enough power, enough memory, enough chips, and enough financing to build the infrastructure?" The models are commoditising; the resources to run them are the bottleneck. This is why the 30-year yield, oil prices, and natural gas prices matter more to your AI portfolio than any single model launch.

5. **A "contrarian bet" of $129M against semiconductor stocks is a signal, but not a prediction.** When one trader makes a huge bet, it could be a hedge (e.g., a fund that owns lots of chip stocks wants to protect against a downturn) or a genuine directional bet. If they are wrong and have to unwind the trade, it could cause a "short squeeze" (a rapid price rise as short sellers are forced to buy back). The lesson: big trades create risk, but don't assume they are "smart money."

## 9. Foundations & Terms

### Concept Spotlight: Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio

**What it is:** The Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio is the most common quick way to judge whether a stock is cheap or expensive. It's simply the stock price divided by the company's earnings per share (EPS) over the last 12 months (or projected for the next 12 months).

**Formula:**
P/E = Stock Price ÷ Earnings Per Share (EPS)

**Why it exists:** Investors need a way to compare the price of a stock to the profit it generates. A $100 stock that earns $10 per share (P/E of 10) is cheaper than a $100 stock that earns $2 per share (P/E of 50). The P/E standardises "price" across companies of different sizes.

**Concrete worked example:**
- Company A: stock price $200, earnings per share $10. P/E = 200/10 = 20.
- Company B: stock price $100, earnings per share $10. P/E = 100/10 = 10.
- Company B is "cheaper" by the P/E measure, even though its stock price is lower. If you pay $100 for Company B, you get a $10 profit per year (a 10% "earnings yield"). If you pay $200 for Company A, you get only $10 profit per year (a 5% yield).

**How it shows up in the news today:**
- The S&P 500 P/E ratio is a frequently cited metric. When the market hits record highs, analysts say "the S&P 500 is trading at a P/E of 25x" — meaning the index price is 25 times the earnings of its constituent companies. That's above the historical average of ~15-18x, which is a sign that stocks are expensive (or that earnings are expected to grow rapidly).
- Today, the market dropped. If the drop is purely price-driven (no change in earnings), the P/E ratio falls — stocks become "cheaper" relative to earnings. But if earnings are also falling (as they might if the economy slows), the P/E might stay the same.

**Rough benchmarks to remember:**
- Historical average S&P 500 P/E: ~15-18x.
- "Growth stocks" (tech, AI) often trade at 30-50x because investors expect future earnings to be much larger than today's.
- "Value stocks" (utilities, banks) often trade at 10-15x.
- A P/E below 10 is often a sign of a distressed company or a cyclical industry at the bottom.
- A P/E above 40 is a sign that the market has very high growth expectations — and that disappointment will be punished hard.

**Caution / common misconception:**
- The P/E ratio is backward-looking (trailing P/E uses past earnings) or forward-looking (forward P/E uses analyst estimates). Both have flaws: past earnings don't guarantee future earnings, and analyst estimates are often wrong.
- A low P/E does not always mean "cheap" — the company may be in a declining industry (e.g., a newspaper chain) where earnings are about to fall.
- A high P/E does not always mean "expensive" — the company may be growing so fast that today's earnings are tiny relative to future earnings (e.g., a young AI company that is not yet profitable).
- The P/E ratio is useless for companies with negative earnings (no P/E at all — it's a division by zero). Many AI companies, especially early-stage ones, are unprofitable, so you can't use P/E to value them.

### Concept Spotlight: Export Controls

**What it is:** Export controls are government restrictions on selling certain goods, technologies, or services to specific countries, companies, or individuals. Unlike tariffs (which are taxes on imports), export controls limit what can leave the country.

**Why they exist:** Export controls are used for national security, foreign policy, or non-proliferation reasons. The idea is: "We don't want our enemies, potential adversaries, or competitors to get access to our most advanced technology because they could use it against us, copy it, or build their own military capabilities."

**The chip-war weapon of choice:** The US uses export controls to restrict the sale of advanced semiconductors (like Nvidia's H100/B200 AI chips), semiconductor manufacturing equipment (like ASML's EUV lithography machines), and software (like chip design tools from Cadence/Synopsys) to China. The goal is to slow China's progress in AI and military technology.

**Concrete worked example:**
- The US government says: "You cannot sell Nvidia's A100 or H100 AI chips to China without a license." Nvidia, a US company, would lose billions of dollars in revenue if it obeyed. But if it didn't obey, it could face fines or even criminal charges.
- Nvidia then designs a "lower-spec" chip (the A800/H800) that complies with the export control rules (slower interconnects, less memory bandwidth) and sells that to China instead. The US government then tightens the rules to also block those chips.
- The result: China cannot buy the most advanced chips. But Chinese companies start designing their own chips (like Huawei's Ascend) and building their own factories (like SMIC), which may eventually close the gap.

**How it shows up in the news today:**
- The Forbes article says export controls harm Nvidia, AMD, and Intel "beyond just lost sales" — because they lose the revenue that funds R&D, and they create a Chinese competitor that will eventually undercut them.
- The entire "AI chip supply chain" is shaped by export controls. TSMC cannot manufacture advanced chips for Chinese AI companies without a license. The US government is also trying to control the flow of chip-making equipment from the Netherlands (ASML) and Japan (Tokyo Electron).

**Rough benchmarks to remember:**
- The US controls about 90% of the global market for AI chips (Nvidia+AMD+Intel).
- China represents about 25-30% of global semiconductor demand.
- Export controls have been tightened five times since 2022, each time adding more restrictions.
- The "TSMC loophole" is that TSMC (Taiwan) is not a US company, but it uses US-origin software and equipment, so the US can still control its exports under the "foreign direct product rule."

**Caution / common misconception:**
- Export controls are not a tariff — they don't raise revenue for the government. They are a denial of business.
- They are hard to enforce because technology is intangible. You can't stop someone from downloading a chip design or from shipping chips through a third country (transshipment).
- They can backfire: if US companies cannot sell to China, they lose market share, and Chinese companies fill the gap. Over time, export controls may weaken the US technology lead rather than preserve it.

### Terms from Today's News

- **Annualized Revenue Run Rate:** A projection of a company's revenue for a full year, based on the most recent month's or quarter's revenue. If a company earned $1B in July, its annualized run rate is $12B (assuming it earns $1B every month for the next 11 months). It's a "if this keeps up" number, not a guarantee. Anthropic used it to imply it is on track for $65B in annual revenue.

- **Memorandum of Understanding (MoU):** A non-binding agreement between two or more parties to explore a business relationship or a deal. It's a "handshake on paper" — it signals intent but does not create a legal obligation to complete the deal. The $500B Nvidia investment consortium uses MOUs, meaning the actual investments are not yet committed.

- **Discount Rate (in finance):** The interest rate used to convert a future dollar of profit into its value today. The logic: $100 earned in 2030 is worth less than $100 today because you could invest $100 today and earn interest. The higher the discount rate (e.g., the 30-year Treasury yield at 5.33%), the less a future profit is worth today.
