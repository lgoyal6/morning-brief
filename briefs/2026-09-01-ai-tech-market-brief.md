**Oil surges past $92 after tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz; Iran and US trade strikes.**  
Global oil prices jumped above $92 a barrel on Tuesday after reports that two tankers were struck in the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway between Iran and Oman through which about 20% of the world's oil passes ([MarketWatch](https://www.marketwatch.com/story/global-oil-prices-extend-move-over-90-after-report-of-two-tankers-struck-in-hormuz-0effd708)). ## 2. Markets, Money & Deals

**10-year Treasury yield hits highest since January 2025 as oil prices fuel inflation worries.**  
The 10-year yield (the interest rate the US government pays to borrow for 10 years, and a benchmark for all other borrowing) rose to levels not seen since January 2025, driven by the surge in oil prices ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/01/bonds-treasury-yields-middle-east-tensions.html)). Higher oil prices push up headline inflation (CPI and PCE), which makes investors expect the Federal Reserve will keep interest rates high — or even raise them. *Why it matters*: Rising yields make borrowing more expensive for everyone: companies, homebuyers, and the government. They also make stocks less attractive compared to bonds (since bonds now pay more). The yield curve (the gap between short-term and long-term yields) is watched closely here.

The model is available for download, so anyone with enough hardware can run it.

**Zhipu's GLM-5.3-Flash undercuts Claude and GPT on price, but not on hardware.**  
Zhipu, a Chinese AI lab, released GLM-5.3-Flash, a model that is priced significantly lower than Anthropic's Claude and OpenAI's GPT for API access ([startupfortune.com](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMingFBVV95cUxNb1g5R2pzNkpRY3VFOFBJeXR1VnBBNmxMbkJUcTNHdzNleUZDR3gzdTd1a0VqdmlURWVsamtnNTZ2dmpWZjZ0UzV2aVkzOXhvTjM4QlJWMmNuQ3FyT3hoR1VTSjBQaWpqRUtzbEhNSmpwdTllYjRRNktWUnVXSy1obDZ1SXlxaHhybzFsTVoyLUNENVFDUHFPVlBBVXZtdw?oc=5)). However, the hardware requirements for running it are still substantial — you need a powerful GPU to run it locally. *Why it matters*: This continues the trend of Chinese labs competing on price. For users who can tolerate higher latency or lower quality, these models offer a cheaper alternative. But the hardware barrier means that for self-hosted use, the total cost of ownership (GPU + electricity) may still be higher than paying for API.

**New paper: "The Halt Vector" — teaching models to stop reasoning earlier.**  
A new research paper from arXiv introduces a technique called "The Halt Vector" that addresses a problem with reasoning models: they often keep generating chain-of-thought even after they have arrived at the correct answer ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.28859)). The authors found that on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, the chain of thought runs about twice as long as needed. Their method "internalizes a causal steering intervention" to make the model stop earlier. *Why it matters*: This could reduce inference costs (since you pay per token) and latency. If applied to commercial models, it could save millions of dollars in compute costs. This is a practical advance, not just a theoretical one.

**Paper: "Time Capsule of Testable Human Knowledge" — 41 years of Jeopardy! in a single local model.**  
Researchers have created a model that can answer Jeopardy! questions from 41 years of the show, all running locally on a single machine ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27459)). This is a modern replication of IBM's Watson, which required a cluster of servers in 2011. *Why it matters*: It demonstrates how far AI has come in terms of compression — a task that once required a supercomputer can now be done on a laptop. This is a proxy for the broader trend of models getting smaller and more efficient.

## 8. Building Your Knowledge

- **How the Strait of Hormuz works as a chokepoint:** The Strait is only 33 km (21 miles) wide at its narrowest point. Ships must pass through Iranian and Omani waters. A single mine or missile can block traffic for days. The 2019 Abqaiq-Khurais attack (on Saudi oil facilities) removed 5.7 million barrels per day of production temporarily, but that was a single incident. The Hormuz chokepoint is more fragile because it is a transit route for 20% of global oil — any disruption immediately affects spot prices. Remember: the market prices in the *risk* of disruption, not just actual disruption. Even a threat of closure can move prices $5-10.
- **Why the Fed focuses on core PCE, not headline CPI, but cannot ignore oil completely:** Core PCE excludes food and energy because they are volatile. But a sustained oil price increase eventually feeds into core inflation through transportation costs, plastics, and chemicals. The Fed's "2% target" is for core PCE, but if oil doubles and stays high, core PCE will eventually rise. The Fed's tolerance for a temporary oil spike is high, but if the spike persists, they will act.
- **The difference between "open-weight" and "open-source" in AI models:** Open-weight means the trained model parameters are released — you can download and run the model. Open-source means the training code, data, and methodology are also released. Many Chinese models are open-weight but not fully open-source (the training data is not released). The Apache 2.0 license (used by Tencent Hy4) is permissive for commercial use, but the MIT license (used by DeepSeek V4) is even more permissive — it allows almost anything, including incorporating into proprietary software.
- **Why Nvidia investing in MediaTek is about more than just chips:** Nvidia's core business is selling GPUs for data centers. But the future of AI includes edge computing — running AI on phones, cars, and IoT devices. MediaTek has a massive distribution network for those markets. The deal is Nvidia's hedge against the risk that data center AI growth slows. It also gives Nvidia a foothold in the automotive AI market, which is expected to grow rapidly.
- **How to think about the "AI capex distorting GDP" argument:** GDP counts all spending on final goods and services. If a company builds a $10 billion data center, that adds $10 billion to GDP today. But the economic value of that data center depends on whether it generates future revenue. If the AI applications built on that data center fail to produce value, the initial GDP boost was a misallocation of resources. This is the difference between "investment" and "consumption" — investment is supposed to create future productive capacity, but it doesn't always. Investors should be skeptical of GDP growth driven by a single sector (AI) unless they see evidence of downstream demand.
- **The significance of CXMT shipping HBM3E to Alibaba and Cambricon:** This is a direct challenge to the US export control regime. The US thought it could cut China off from advanced memory by restricting sales of equipment and technology to Chinese memory makers. CXMT's success suggests that either the controls have loopholes, or China has developed its own technology. For investors in SK Hynix (not on the watchlist but a major memory supplier), this is a competitive threat. For investors in US chip equipment makers (AMAT, KLAC), it could mean that Chinese buyers are less dependent on them, reducing future orders.

## 9. Foundations & Terms

### Concept Spotlight: Credit Spread (Markets/Investing)

**What it is in plain words:** A credit spread is the difference in yield (the interest rate) between two bonds, usually a corporate bond and a government bond of the same maturity. It measures how much extra compensation investors demand to hold a riskier bond instead of a "risk-free" government bond.

**Why it exists / what problem it solves:** Government bonds (like US Treasuries) are considered nearly risk-free because the US government is very unlikely to default. Corporate bonds carry risk of default (the company might go bankrupt). Investors need to be paid extra for taking that risk. The credit spread is that extra payment. It also reflects the market's view of the economy: if investors are worried about defaults, they demand higher spreads.

**Concrete worked example with real numbers:** Suppose a 10-year US Treasury bond yields 5.0%. A 10-year bond from a company with a "BBB" credit rating (investment grade, but not the safest) yields 6.5%. The credit spread is 1.5 percentage points, or 150 basis points (100 bps = 1 percentage point). If the economy weakens and investors fear that company might default, the spread could widen to 3.0% (300 bps). If the economy strengthens, the spread could narrow to 1.0% (100 bps).

**How it shows up in today's news:** Today, the 10-year Treasury yield rose to its highest since January 2025. But credit spreads themselves were not directly mentioned. However, the rise in yields is partly driven by the same factors that affect credit spreads: inflation fears and the risk of higher interest rates. When the Fed raises rates, it increases the risk of default for companies with weak finances, which widens credit spreads. Investors in corporate bond ETFs (like LQD or HYG) watch credit spreads closely.

**Rough benchmarks worth remembering:** 
- Investment grade (BBB-rated) spreads: typically 100-200 bps over Treasuries.
- High-yield (junk bond, BB-rated and below) spreads: typically 300-500 bps, but can widen to 1000+ bps during recessions.
- The 2008 financial crisis saw high-yield spreads peak at over 2000 bps (20% extra yield).

**Caution or common misconception:** A narrow credit spread does not mean there is no risk — it means the market is not demanding much extra compensation. That can be a bubble. A wide spread does not necessarily mean a company is about to default — it could be a temporary panic. The spread is a market signal, not a fact about the company's health.

### Concept Spotlight: Sovereignty and Recognition (World Affairs)

**What it is in plain words:** Sovereignty means a government has the ultimate authority over its territory and people. Recognition is when other countries formally accept that government as the legitimate ruler of that territory. A country can have sovereignty in practice (it controls its land) but lack recognition (other countries do not officially deal with it).

**Why it exists / what problem it solves:** The international system is based on the idea that each country is a sovereign state, and no country can interfere in another's internal affairs. But this is only true if other countries respect that sovereignty. Recognition is the diplomatic tool that confers legitimacy. Without recognition, a government cannot borrow money from international markets, sign treaties, or join international organizations.

**Concrete worked example with real numbers:** Taiwan has full sovereignty over its territory (the government controls the island, collects taxes, has a military, etc.). But only about 12 countries formally recognize Taiwan as a sovereign state. Most countries, including the US, recognize the "One China" policy and do not have formal diplomatic relations with Taiwan. Yet Taiwan is a major economic power — it is the world's largest maker of advanced chips. The US maintains unofficial relations through the American Institute in Taiwan. This is a workaround: Taiwan has de facto sovereignty but not de jure recognition.

**How it shows up in today's news:** In today's brief, we saw that China is trying to push Taiwan out of the Pacific Islands Forum (a diplomatic body). This is a fight over recognition — China wants to isolate Taiwan internationally. Similarly, Brazil's accusations of US meddling in its election are about sovereignty — the US is accused of violating Brazil's right to conduct its own affairs. The conflict in Ukraine is also about sovereignty: Russia argues that Crimea and other territories are now part of Russia, but most countries do not recognize the annexation.

**Rough benchmarks worth remembering:**
- The UN has 193 member states. Taiwan is not a member.
- The number of countries that recognize Taiwan has fluctuated; it was over 70 in the 1970s, now down to about 12.
- Kosovo is recognized by about 100 countries but not by Serbia, Russia, China, and some others.
- Palestine is recognized by 139 countries but is not a UN member (it has observer status).

**Caution or common misconception:** Sovereignty is not absolute. Countries can be sovereign in theory but heavily influenced by a more powerful neighbor (this is called a "sphere of influence"). Also, a government can lose recognition if it is overthrown by a coup — other countries may choose to recognize the new government or not.

### Other Terms from Today's News

- **HBM3E:** High-Bandwidth Memory generation 3E — the latest standard for memory used in AI accelerators. It stacks memory chips vertically to provide high bandwidth while saving space. HBM is critical for AI training because GPUs need to access large amounts of data quickly. The "E" stands for "extended" — a version with higher speeds than HBM3.
- **Apache 2.0 License:** A permissive open-source software license that allows users to use, modify, distribute, and even sell software that uses the licensed code, as long as they include the original copyright notice. It is less restrictive than the GPL (which requires derivative works to also be open source) and similar to the MIT license.
- **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE):** A model architecture where only a subset of the model's parameters (the "experts") is activated for each input. For example, Tencent Hy4 has 770 billion total parameters but only 49 billion active. This means the model is large and capable, but inference is fast and cheap because most of the network is "asleep." MoE is the key to models like GPT-4, DeepSeek V4, and Tencent Hy4.
- **Context window:** The maximum number of tokens (words or parts of words) a model can process at once. A 1 million token context window means the model can read a whole novel (about 750,000 words) in one go. This is useful for analyzing long documents, codebases, or conversations. Most models have 8,000 to 128,000 tokens.
- **Shadow Fleet:** A term not used today, but relevant to oil tanker attacks. A shadow fleet is a group of tankers that operate in secrecy, often with insurance and ownership hidden, to avoid sanctions. Iran uses shadow fleets to export oil despite US sanctions. The tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz may involve shadow fleet vessels.
- **Government shutdown:** When Congress fails to pass funding bills, the US government must stop non-essential operations. Essential services (military, air traffic control, etc.) continue, but national parks close, some employees are furloughed, and economic data releases may be delayed. A shutdown is politically damaging and can hurt the economy.
- **Continuing resolution (CR):** A temporary spending bill that keeps the government funded at current levels for a short period, usually to give Congress more time to negotiate a full budget. The House is voting on a CR that would extend until after the midterm elections.
- **Personal sanctions:** When a country imposes sanctions on specific individuals rather than entire countries. For example, the US has personal sanctions on Russian Finance Minister Siluanov, but he was allowed to attend the G20 meeting anyway. This shows that the US is willing to waive sanctions for diplomatic purposes.
- **G20:** The Group of 20, a forum of the world's 20 largest economies (including the EU). It meets annually to discuss global economic issues. The finance ministers' meeting is a precursor to the leaders' summit.
- **Midterm elections:** The US elections for Congress that occur halfway through a president's term. They are held in November 2026. All 435 House seats and about one-third of Senate seats are up for election. The president's party typically loses seats in midterms. The Iran situation and the economy will be major issues.
- **Frozen conflict:** A conflict that has ended with a ceasefire but no peace treaty, and the underlying issues remain unresolved. The Russia-Ukraine war could become a frozen conflict if fighting stops but no agreement is reached on territory, as in the case of Transnistria (Moldova) or the Korean Peninsula.
